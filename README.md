## Abstract

Debates about artificial intelligence (AI) and the knowledge commons have concentrated on the appropriation of training data and on restricted access. This paper argues that a further enclosure concerns the governance of knowledge: who sets the standards by which contributions are selected, justified and recognised, and to whom those standards answer.

Drawing on the history of citation indexing and on social epistemology, the paper separates the origin of a claim, its evidential justification, the justification of the standards that selected it, and the authority that makes it count. Answerability is relative to those affected, and concentration is not enclosure. The third enclosure occurs when unanswerable epistemic intermediation materially displaces a community’s effective capacity to sustain and contest the epistemic practices on which it depends: by transfer, when a system’s standards come to govern a practice and those it serves cannot stop them, or by suppression, when reliance at scale depletes a valuable, contestably governed public practice into an unanswerable substitute on which they come to depend. Its strict case is the displacement of an epistemic commons. The Science Citation Index shows external metrics entering research evaluation and perhaps, in places, governing it; declining contributions to public question-and-answer forums show a plausible mechanism of suppression. Drawing on Helen Longino, the paper argues that such displacement can be an epistemic loss as well as a political one. AI makes these conditions possible across general-purpose knowledge infrastructure; how far they have produced displacement is an empirical question. A companion paper sets out the remedies.

## From one paper to two

Version 1.0 (November 2025), *The Third Enclosure Movement: Rethinking AI Regulation through a Taxonomy of Restricted Knowledge*, was a public release of the author’s Master of Laws (LLM) dissertation at the University of Reading. It combined a theory of enclosure, a comparative legal analysis of the United Kingdom, European Union, United States and China, sector case studies, and proposals for reform.

Revising it produced two papers:

1. **The Third Enclosure Movement: Provenance, Answerability and Recognition in Artificial Intelligence** (this repository). A full rewrite as philosophy of AI. It keeps the version 1.0 taxonomy of restricted access, but moves the central claim from restricted access to the governance of epistemic standards — who sets the standards by which knowledge is selected and recognised, and to whom they answer.
2. **The Answerable Commons: Governing the Epistemic Standards of AI Intermediaries** (companion paper). It takes over the legal analysis and the remedies, including version 1.0’s partial-commons reforms, and the comparative legal audit of the United Kingdom, European Union, United States and China.
